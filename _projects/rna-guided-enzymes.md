CRISPR enzymes are usually described as DNA cutters. That is a fact about their
architecture, not a constraint: the same RNA-guided scaffold can be pointed at
other substrates once the parts that enforce double-stranded DNA specificity are
taken away.

IscB is the compact ancestral relative of Cas9. Removing its target-interacting
domain converts both IscB and Cas9 into RNA-guided RNA editors, which yields a
programmable RNA-targeting enzyme small enough to deliver easily
{% cite xu2025conversion %}.

To understand why that truncation works, we determined cryo-EM structures of the
engineered enzymes bound to single-stranded DNA and RNA targets, captured in both
seed-duplexed and fully duplexed states {% cite xu2026structure %}. The structures
show how the ωRNA guide accommodates a single-stranded target, and they identify
the contacts that limit activity in cells.

Two directions follow from this. The first is therapeutic: the expanded CAG and
CTG repeats behind Huntington's disease and related disorders form
single-stranded slip-out structures, which are exactly the substrate these
engineered enzymes prefer. The second is a measurement tool: fusing an
RNA-guided enzyme to a base editor turns it into a locus-specific probe for
where single-stranded DNA becomes exposed across the genome.
